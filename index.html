Home — About Me

Hi, I'm Amir.

I'm a third-year Computer Science for Software Development student at York University, currently working as an undergraduate researcher and building a career around software that's efficient, private, and open.

I grew up in Tehran, Iran, in a family that pushed me toward academically demanding STEM schooling from an early age — an environment that taught me to enjoy, rather than fear, difficult subjects. That early exposure to mathematics and logic is what eventually pulled me toward computer science: a field that lets me combine analytical thinking with building things people actually use.

I'm currently enrolled in the Computer Science for Software Development program at York University's Markham campus, expecting to graduate in May 2028. Alongside coursework in data structures, algorithms, and object-oriented design, I work as an undergraduate researcher studying performance modelling and AI-assisted software engineering tools.

Outside of research and coursework, I build small projects that reflect what I care about most in software: a chess engine focused on clean architecture and testing, and a password manager focused on secure, efficient data handling. Both are open source.

"If people never did silly things nothing intelligent would ever get done." — Ludwig Wittgenstein

This quote has stuck with me because it reframes failed experiments as a required part of getting to a good idea, not a detour from it — which is exactly how research and debugging actually feel.

What I'm working toward

I want to work on systems people depend on without having to blindly trust them — software that's efficient enough to be practical, private enough to be safe, and open enough to be verified rather than simply believed. That belief shows up directly in how I build: my password manager exists because I think privacy tooling shouldn't be gatekept, and my chess engine exists because I think efficient software shouldn't require expensive hardware to be usable. I want a career built around putting people ahead of profit — technology that's genuinely accessible, not just marketed that way.
