Goal: Discover Myself

Everything in this section is organized around one broader goal — understanding what I want out of a career and what's actually driving that, then building toward it deliberately rather than by default.

Portfolio: Dreams & Goals

I always knew I wanted to study something mathematically oriented. Growing up in academically intensive private schools in Tehran gave me an early, sustained exposure to STEM subjects, and by the time I was choosing a major, computer science was the natural landing point — it let me combine a liking for logic and programming with genuinely difficult problem-solving.

Long-term, I want to be a computer science researcher: work that's independent, intellectually engaging, and lets me build systems that are efficient, private, and open by default rather than as an afterthought. That vision — spelled out in full on my home page — is what most of the goals below are actually in service of.

Portfolio: Interests & Skills

Personal development goals, written using the SMART framework, organized by competency.

Communication Skills

Pure technical skill isn't enough — the gap between a talented developer and an extraordinary one is usually the ability to explain complex ideas clearly to people with different backgrounds. My weekly research reports and presentations are where I'm working on this directly.

Goal 1

Written communication. By August 31, 2026, complete Coursera's Microsoft professional writing course one lesson at a time, applying each lesson to my weekly reports, and score at least 80% on my next two written course assignments in the Fall.

Goal 2

Oral communication. By July 15, 2026, request feedback after each of four weekly co-op presentations and apply at least one improvement to the next presentation each time.

Portfolio: Values & Personality

My values assessment results weren't surprising once I looked at them: I ranked high income, real opportunities for salary growth, job security, intellectually stimulating and creative work, low pressure, helping others, work-life balance, and independence as the values that matter most to me professionally — which lines up almost exactly with why I picked computer science in the first place.

My lifestyle values point the same direction: owning a home, living somewhere safe, time with family and friends, creative hobbies, and room for arts and entertainment all ranked highly, which tells me I care just as much about life outside of work as I do about the work itself.

One value I noticed was conspicuously missing from the results was my love of learning — something intellectually stimulating work doesn't automatically capture, since it doesn't necessarily mean constantly picking up new topics on my own. I also noticed a few direct tensions in my own rankings: wanting a high income but low pressure, and wanting independence but also job security. I don't think those tensions resolve cleanly — I think the honest answer is that the career I'm aiming for, computer science research, is my attempt to hold all of them at once.
